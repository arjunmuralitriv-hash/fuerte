# Qatar's NIA standard explained

**Short answer:** The National Information Assurance (NIA) standard is Qatar's national baseline for protecting information. It is owned by the National Cyber Security Agency (NCSA). Government entities and critical-sector organisations are expected to comply and to prove it through an audit. Many private companies that serve them are asked to follow it too. To start, classify your information, assess your gaps against the controls, fix the highest risks first and keep evidence.

> **Disclaimer:** This guide is general information, not legal or certification advice. Confirm your obligations with NCSA, your sector regulator or Qatari counsel.

## What is the NIA standard?

The NIA standard sets out how organisations in Qatar should govern and protect their information and systems. It was first issued as the National Information Assurance Policy and is now maintained by NCSA. It works alongside the National Data Classification Policy, which tells you how sensitive each piece of information is, and therefore how strong its protection must be. [CONFIRM: classification levels and their names.]

## Who has to comply?

- **Government entities:** ministries, authorities and other public bodies.
- **Critical sectors:** organisations that run services Qatar depends on, such as energy, utilities, telecommunications, healthcare, transport and finance. [CONFIRM: the sectors NCSA lists as in scope.]
- **Their suppliers:** contractors, integrators and service providers that handle these organisations' information or connect to their systems are often required by contract to meet the same controls.

Other private companies are not usually required to comply, but many use the standard as a practical security baseline. [CONFIRM: applicability wording with NCSA guidance.]

## How is the standard structured?

The standard has two layers:

- **Governance controls:** information security leadership, policies, risk management, asset management, classification, third-party security, awareness training, incident management and business continuity.
- **Technical controls:** access control, network and gateway security, cryptography, logging and monitoring, secure configuration, physical and environmental security, and the security of cloud and virtual environments.

The controls you need depend on the classification of the information a system holds. More sensitive information calls for more controls.

## How does compliance and certification work?

1. **Define the scope:** the organisation, systems and information covered.
2. **Prepare documentation:** policies, risk assessments and evidence that each control works.
3. **Audit:** an assessment by an auditor accredited under NCSA's certification scheme. [CONFIRM: the current certification scheme and accreditation process.]
4. **Certification:** NCSA decides on certification based on the audit.
5. **Keep it current:** compliance is reviewed periodically. [CONFIRM: audit frequency, e.g. annual.]

## How does NIA relate to ISO 27001 and the PDPPL?

- **ISO 27001** is an international standard with a similar structure: a management system plus a catalogue of controls. Work done for ISO 27001 often covers much of the NIA standard, but the NIA standard has Qatar-specific requirements, such as national classification, so one certificate does not replace the other. [CONFIRM: whether NCSA recognises ISO 27001 for any part of NIA compliance.]
- **The PDPPL** (Law No. 13 of 2016) protects personal data. The NIA standard protects information of every kind. If you hold personal data, you need both. See our [PDPPL compliance checklist](/insights/pdppl-compliance-checklist-qatar).

## Where to start: a practical checklist

### 1. Find out whether you are in scope
Check whether you are a government entity, in a critical sector, or a supplier whose contract requires the NIA standard. Ask your regulator or client if unsure.

### 2. Classify your information
Use the National Data Classification Policy to label your information and the systems that hold it. Classification drives every later decision.

### 3. Assess your gaps
Compare your current controls with the standard, starting with the systems that hold your most sensitive information. Record each gap with its risk.

### 4. Fix the highest risks first
Typical early fixes are access control and multi-factor authentication, network segmentation, logging and monitoring, patching, and backups you have tested.

### 5. Write it down and train people
Approve the policies, assign owners, and train staff on their responsibilities. Auditors look for evidence that controls work in practice, not just on paper.

### 6. Monitor and improve
Track incidents, review risks regularly and test your controls, for example with penetration testing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the NIA standard mandatory for private companies?
Usually not, unless you are in a critical sector or your contract with a government or critical-sector client requires it. [CONFIRM: applicability with NCSA guidance.]

### Is ISO 27001 enough for NIA compliance?
Not on its own. ISO 27001 covers much of the same ground, but the NIA standard adds Qatar-specific requirements, so plan a gap assessment against the NIA controls.

### How long does it take to comply?
It depends on your scope and starting point. A focused gap assessment can take a few weeks; closing the gaps and preparing for an audit usually takes several months.

### Does the NIA standard apply to cloud services?
Yes. The standard includes controls for cloud and virtual environments, and the classification of your information affects where it may be hosted. [CONFIRM: current NCSA cloud security requirements.]
